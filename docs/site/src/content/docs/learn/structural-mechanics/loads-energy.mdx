## How far will the end move?

A straight bar is fixed at its left end and pulled to the right by a force $F$.
If the material is twice as stiff, does the support reaction double? Does the
extension halve? We can answer both questions before constructing a numerical
model.

Assume a uniform cross-section $A$, length $L$, an axial load through its center,
and slow loading so acceleration is negligible. We describe the interior away
from the detailed grip by uniform axial stress. Strains are small, the material
is homogeneous, and its axial response is linear elastic.

## Three different ingredients

**Equilibrium.** Taking rightward forces as positive, the complete bar satisfies
$R+F=0$. Cut it at a position $x$. The tensile internal force $N(x)$ must balance
the end load, so $N=F$ throughout the bar. The axial stress is
$\sigma=N/A=F/A$, measured in pascals: $1\,\mathrm{Pa}=1\,\mathrm{N/m^2}$.

**Kinematics.** Let $u(x)$ be displacement in metres, with $u(0)=0$. Two nearby
points initially separated by $\mathrm{d}x$ acquire a separation change
$u'(x)\mathrm{d}x$. The axial strain is therefore $\varepsilon=u'(x)$, a
dimensionless ratio.

**Material law.** For the chosen linear elastic material,
$\sigma=E\varepsilon$, with Young's modulus $E$ in pascals. Equilibrium alone
cannot supply $E$ or decide whether this law fits a specimen. These scalar
definitions and Hooke's law are developed in Roylance's
[Introduction to Elasticity, pp. 4–6](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/3-11-mechanics-of-materials-fall-1999/e8b60e8a1efb6e7275372cc77f23ae9b_MIT3_11F99_elas_1.pdf).

Combining the three ingredients gives

$$
EAu'(x)=F,\qquad
u(x)=\frac{F}{EA}x,\qquad
\delta=u(L)=\frac{FL}{EA}.
$$

The equivalent spring stiffness is $k=EA/L$, in N/m. It depends on geometry
as well as material. Doubling $E$ halves $\delta$ under the same applied $F$,
but leaves $R=-F$ unchanged. By contrast, if the end displacement $\delta$ is
prescribed, the required force $F=EA\delta/L$ doubles.

That distinction is the mechanical form of
[prescribed values versus natural boundary data](/learn/mathematical-modeling/boundary-interface-conditions/).
Changing which quantity is controlled changes the experiment.

## An independent numerical prediction

Choose illustrative values, not a material specification:

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| $L$ | $2\,\mathrm{m}$ |
| $A$ | $100\,\mathrm{mm^2}=10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}$ |
| $E$ | $200\,\mathrm{GPa}=2\times10^{11}\,\mathrm{Pa}$ |
| $F$ | $1000\,\mathrm{N}$ |

Then $EA=2\times10^7\,\mathrm{N}$, $\sigma=10\,\mathrm{MPa}$,
$\varepsilon=5\times10^{-5}$ and

$$
\delta=10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m}=0.1\,\mathrm{mm},\qquad R=-1000\,\mathrm{N}.
$$

The extension is tiny compared with the bar length, consistent with the
small-strain approximation. This calculation says nothing about a material's
yield stress: stiffness and strength answer different questions.

## Energy gives the same answer

For a spring loaded gradually from zero, the force rises from zero to $k\delta$.
The work is the area under that force–displacement line:

$$
U=\int_0^\delta k s\,\mathrm{d}s=\frac12k\delta^2.
$$

At equilibrium this is $F\delta/2=0.05\,\mathrm{J}$ for our bar. The final
force multiplied by the final displacement, $F\delta$, is twice the stored
energy; it is not the work done during gradual loading.

For a fixed external force, form the total potential energy

$$
\Pi(\delta)=\frac12k\delta^2-F\delta.
$$

A small admissible change $\eta$ gives
$\Pi'(\delta)\eta=(k\delta-F)\eta$. Requiring this to vanish for every
$\eta$ recovers $k\delta=F$. Because $k>0$, the second derivative is positive:
equilibrium minimizes this potential. This is the discrete starting point for
the virtual-work formulation in
[chapter 3](/learn/structural-mechanics/virtual-work/); see also Bathe's
[linear-analysis lecture 1](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-2-002-finite-element-procedures-for-solids-and-structures-spring-2010/resources/lecture-1/).

## Replace an end load by a distributed load

Let $p$ be a constant axial force per unit length, in N/m, and make the right end
free. A small segment gives $N'(x)+p=0$. With $N(L)=0$,

$$
N(x)=p(L-x),\qquad
u(x)=\frac{p}{EA}\left(Lx-\frac{x^2}{2}\right),\qquad
R=-pL.
$$

Stress now falls toward the free end, while displacement grows with a decreasing
slope. This quadratic shape is exactly the idea behind the two-dimensional
square we will run later. A uniform *body* load is not the same as a load applied
only at the tip.

## What goes wrong if a support is missing?

If neither end fixes displacement, adding a constant $c$ to $u(x)$ changes no
strain and costs no elastic energy. A balanced load can determine extension
without determining absolute position. An unbalanced load cannot have a static
equilibrium at all. A singular stiffness matrix can be a faithful report of a
missing physical constraint.

Likewise, fixing both ends while prescribing a nonzero extension would impose
incompatible data. Adding restraints until a solve succeeds is not a substitute
for deciding how the body is actually held.

## Exercises

1. Double the cross-sectional area under the same end force. Predict stress,
   strain, extension and reaction before substituting numbers.
2. Prescribe $\delta=0.2\,\mathrm{mm}$ for the numerical bar instead of $F$.
   Find the new force and energy. **Check:** $2000\,\mathrm{N}$ and $0.2\,\mathrm{J}$.
3. For the uniform distributed load, integrate $N(x)^2/(2EA)$ to find the stored
   energy. **Check:** $p^2L^3/(6EA)$.
4. Explain why replacing that distributed load by an end force $pL$ preserves
   the reaction but doubles the end displacement.
5. A plot shows an extension of one tenth of the bar length. Which physical
   assumption would you revisit before interpreting the stress?
