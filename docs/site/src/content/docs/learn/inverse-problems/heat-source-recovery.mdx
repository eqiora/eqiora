## Can a warm field reveal its source?

Consider a square conductor with a spatially varying internal heat source and
all four edges held at one temperature. At steady state, the generated heat must
leave through the boundary. Fourier conduction gives a heat flux
$\boldsymbol q=-k\nabla T$ and steady balance gives
$\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol q=q_v$. Thus $-\nabla\cdot(k\nabla T)=q_v$.
The [heat-transfer book](/learn/heat-transfer/) develops these physical ingredients.

Choose a reference temperature $T_{\mathrm{ref}}$, a positive temperature-difference
scale $T_*$, and a reference conductivity $k_*$. Write
$T=T_{\mathrm{ref}}+T_*u$ and $k=k_*a$. For a source
$q_v=k_*T_*s\sin(wx)\sin(wy)$, our dimensionless temperature $u$ and
positive dimensionless conductivity $a$ satisfy

$$
-a\Delta u=s\sin(wx)\sin(wy),\qquad u=b\text{ on the boundary}.
$$

The square has side $L=1\,\mathrm m$, $w=\pi/L$, source scale $s$ has units
$\mathrm m^{-2}$, and the boundary value $b$ is dimensionless. Constant $a$
and a sinusoidal source make a hand solution possible; these are explicit
assumptions of this example.

## Solve before fitting

Try $u=b+A\sin(wx)\sin(wy)$. Each second derivative contributes $-w^2$,
so substitution gives $2aw^2A=s$. Therefore

$$
u(x,y)=b+\frac{s}{2aw^2}\sin(wx)\sin(wy).
$$

With $a=1$, $b=0$, and $s=2\pi^2\,\mathrm m^{-2}$, the centre value is 1.
Doubling $s$ doubles every temperature excess above $b$. Doubling $a$ halves it.
These predictions follow directly from the equation.

Suppose $a$ and $b$ are known and point measurements are $d_i$. Define
$g_i=\sin(wx_i)\sin(wy_i)/(2aw^2)$. The model predicts $b+sg_i$.
Minimizing the sum of squared differences gives

$$
\Phi(s)=\frac12\sum_i(b+sg_i-d_i)^2,\quad
\Phi'(s)=\sum_i g_i(b+sg_i-d_i),
$$

and hence

$$
\widehat s=\frac{\sum_i g_i(d_i-b)}{\sum_i g_i^2}.
$$

The denominator must be positive. Sampling only boundary points makes every
$g_i$ zero: those data cannot recover the source. This scalar calculation is a
worked least-squares problem; the general formulation is in Boyd and
Vandenberghe, chapter 12 of
[*Introduction to Applied Linear Algebra*](https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/vmls/).

## Run the recovery experiment

Use the environment from [Get started](/get-started/). Save
<a href={modelDownload} download="inverse-poisson.eqi">inverse-poisson.eqi</a> and
<a href={programDownload} download="learn_inverse_poisson.py">learn_inverse_poisson.py</a>
beside `.venv`, then run:

```bash
uv run --no-project --python .venv/bin/python python learn_inverse_poisson.py inverse-poisson.eqi
```

The script chooses Q1 finite elements, computes a baseline response, and generates
synthetic data with source $1.4(2\pi^2)$. It applies the scalar formula above to
the computed response vector. Expect a recovered source close to
$27.6348923\,\mathrm m^{-2}$. No iterative optimization is needed for this
one-parameter linear problem.

are two different questions.

## Reuse what you have derived

The Python calls to [`eqiora.diff`](/reference/python/diff/) reuse the compiled
component to evaluate new parameter points and derivatives. There is no need
to write a second Poisson solver or recopy the balance for each candidate source.
The next chapters explain the additional derivative lines already in the script.

## Exercises

1. A centre observation is 0.6 with $a=2$ and $b=0.1$. Recover $s$.
2. Derive the area mean $\bar u=b+2sL^2/(a\pi^4)$ for this one-metre square.
3. Replace $b=0$ by a nonzero known boundary value in the analytic fit. Why must
   you subtract it before fitting the source?
4. Repeat the script with 6, 12, and 24 cells per direction. Record recovery error
   and continuum-mean error separately. Explain why their sizes differ.

**Check your reasoning:** exercise 1 gives $s=2\pi^2\,\mathrm m^{-2}$.
