## A signal has fallen to half its initial value

You observe a signal one second after starting an experiment. Its initial value
was 1 and its new value is 0.5. How fast is it decaying?

A **forward problem** starts with a rate and predicts the signal. An **inverse
problem** starts with the signal and asks about the rate. Both need the same
physical model. Here we assume the rate is constant, the signal is proportional
to the state, and the instrument has no unknown offset.

The model says that the loss per unit time is proportional to the amount present:

$$
\frac{dx}{dt}=-kx,\qquad x(0)=x_0,\qquad k>0.
$$

The state $x$ is dimensionless, time $t$ is in seconds, and $k$ is in inverse
seconds. Dividing by $x$ and integrating gives

$$
\log(x/x_0)=-kt,\qquad x(t)=x_0e^{-kt}.
$$

We have derived the forward answer before touching a solver. The
[ODE lesson](/learn/mathematical-modeling/ordinary-differential-equations/)
explores the evolution equation in more detail.

## Turn the equation around

For one observation $d$ at a known positive time $t$, with known $x_0$,

$$
k=-\frac{1}{t}\log\left(\frac{d}{x_0}\right).
$$

Our observation gives $k=\log 2\approx0.693147\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$.
The formula requires $d>0$. Under the assumed decay model, $d>x_0$ would produce
a negative fitted rate and therefore contradict the assumption of decay.

This is an unusually simple inverse problem: the answer is explicit. It is still
worth stating the components separately.

| Role | This experiment |
| --- | --- |
| Unknown parameter | $k$ |
| State | $x(t;k)$ |
| Observation rule | Read $x$ at $t=1\,\mathrm{s}$ |
| Data | $d=0.5$ |
| Objective | $\tfrac12[x(1;k)-d]^2$ |

An observation rule can instead average a region, sample several times, or
convert a physical quantity to instrument units. Keeping that rule explicit
prevents fitting a model output that the instrument never measured. For the
linear algebra of observations and data fitting, see Boyd and Vandenberghe,
*Introduction to Applied Linear Algebra*, chapters 7 and 13, in the
[authors' edition](https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/vmls/).


Predict the printed values first: about $0.840896$ at $0.25$ seconds,
$0.707107$ at $0.5$ seconds, and $0.5$ at one second. The
<ExactSourceLink path="examples/python/textbook_decay.py" kind="blob">shared run script</ExactSourceLink>
handles compilation, time integration, and sampling; the equation remains in
`.eqi`.

The final sample was used to choose $k$. The earlier samples are additional
predictions. If independently measured values disagree with them, changing $k$
to match one observation more closely will not necessarily repair the model.
An unknown initial condition, an instrument offset, or a changing rate may matter.

## What if the initial value is unknown?

One observation now obeys $d=x_0e^{-kt}$. For every chosen $k$ there is an
$x_0=de^{kt}$ producing exactly that observation. A good fit cannot select between
them. Two positive measurements at distinct times allow the ratio

$$
\frac{d_2}{d_1}=e^{-k(t_2-t_1)},\qquad
k=-\frac{\log(d_2/d_1)}{t_2-t_1}.
$$

This removes $x_0$, after which either observation determines it. The result
assumes the two samples share one initial condition and one constant rate.

## Exercises

1. Recover $k$ when $x_0=2$ and $x(3\,\mathrm{s})=0.25$. Give its units.
2. Fit $k$ from $x(1)=0.5$ and predict $x(2)$. Would a new measurement of 0.4
   agree with this model? Name one additional experiment that could help explain it.
3. With unknown $x_0$, use $d_1=0.8$ at one second and $d_2=0.4$ at three seconds
   to recover both parameters.
4. Differentiate the one-observation estimate with respect to $d$. Explain why
   a small absolute measurement error becomes troublesome near zero.

**Check your reasoning:** exercise 1 gives $k=\log 8/3$; exercise 3 gives
$k=\log 2/2$ and $x_0=0.8\sqrt{2}$.
