## Two explanations for one temperature field

Suppose you observe the entire steady field in the previous chapter. Can you
recover both the source strength $s$ and conductivity $a$?

Recall the solution on the one-metre square:

$$
u=b+\frac{sL^2}{2a\pi^2}\,\psi,\qquad
\psi(x,y)=\sin(\pi x/L)\sin(\pi y/L),
$$

where $L=1\,\mathrm m$. The response depends on the
ratio $s/a$. Multiplying both parameters by the same positive factor leaves
$u$ unchanged everywhere. More temperature sensors do not remove this particular
ambiguity, even with exact measurements.

| Source scale $s$ in $\mathrm m^{-2}$ | Conductivity ratio $a$ | Centre temperature, $b=0$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $2\pi^2$ | 1 | 1 |
| $4\pi^2$ | 2 | 1 |
| $\pi^2$ | 0.5 | 1 |

These are analytically identical predictions. A numerical search can return one
row with an excellent fit while the others remain equally compatible with the data.

## Sensitivity asks about a small change

A parameter sensitivity is the derivative of the observation with respect to a
parameter. At fixed position,

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial s}=\frac{L^2\psi}{2a\pi^2},\qquad
\frac{\partial u}{\partial a}=-\frac{sL^2\psi}{2a^2\pi^2},\qquad
\frac{\partial u}{\partial b}=1.
$$

Let $J$ collect these derivatives for all observed positions. For a small
parameter change $\delta p$,

$$
\delta y\approx J\delta p.
$$

The source and conductivity columns are proportional:
$J_a=-(s/a)J_s$. In direction $\delta p=(s,a,0)$, they cancel. The measurements
have no first-order response in that direction, consistent with the exact
scaling ambiguity above.

**Identifiability** asks whether observations distinguish the unknown parameters.
Column dependence exposes a local ambiguity in a differentiable model. Here we
also have the stronger, explicit family of exactly equivalent solutions.
The linear-dependence language is developed in Boyd and Vandenberghe, chapter 5
of [*Introduction to Applied Linear Algebra*](https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/vmls/).

## Try the cancellation in Eqiora

The [Poisson script](/learn/inverse-problems/heat-source-recovery/#run-the-recovery-experiment)
orders its inputs as source, conductivity, boundary value. Replace its `direction`
array with:

```python
direction = np.array([2.0 * np.pi**2, 1.0, 0.0], dtype=np.float64)
```

The `program.jvp(direction)` call returns the field change in that direction.
Predict a near-zero tangent. Compare it with the source-only direction
`np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0], dtype=np.float64)`, which produces a nonzero field
change in the interior. Both experiments reuse the same compiled mathematical
component and the same numerical plan.

For a finite change, compare `program.evaluate(nominal)` with
`program.evaluate(np.array([4.0 * np.pi**2, 2.0, 0.0], dtype=np.float64))`.
Their primary fields should agree up to the solution error.

## Design an observation that adds information

Which change helps? An independent conductivity measurement fixes $a$, so the
field can determine $s$. Alternatively measure heat flux as well as temperature.
In our normalized model the flux is $\boldsymbol q=-a\nabla u$, so substitution
gives

$$
\boldsymbol q=-\frac{sL^2}{2\pi^2}\nabla\psi.
$$

Its dependence on $a$ has cancelled. A suitably located nonzero flux measurement
can determine $s$, while temperature then determines $a$. A flux measurement at
the centre is unhelpful because the gradient vanishes there. The instrument and
its placement matter as much as the number of readings.

If $b$ is also unknown, include information that separates a uniform offset from
the sinusoidal response. A boundary-temperature reading directly observes $b$;
two distinct interior response levels can also separate the offset and amplitude.
Repeating the centre reading reduces random noise but does not create a second
spatial response pattern.

3. At $(x,y)=(0,0.5)$, calculate the normalized outward flux from the analytic
