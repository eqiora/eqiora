## Switching on the heater

Start the square at 300 K everywhere. At time zero, switch on uniform heating
while holding its exterior at 300 K. Initially there is no temperature gradient.
Energy accumulates in the interior; gradients develop; conduction increasingly
carries energy to the bath. Eventually storage becomes negligible and the
steady solution from the previous chapter is approached.

The governing equation and initial condition are

$$
c_v\partial_tT-\nabla\cdot(k\nabla T)=s,
\qquad T(\boldsymbol x,0)=300\,\mathrm K.
$$

The exterior condition remains $T=300$ K. Initial data describe the starting
thermal state; boundary data describe its surroundings for all subsequent
times. We take $c_v>0$ constant, so storage is linear in temperature.

This chapter connects that physical warming to the actual time-dependent model
and to the approximation introduced by a time step.

## A time scale and a simple exact mode

The ratio $\alpha=k/c_v$ sets the diffusion scale. In a source-free slab with
ends held at $T_b$, try the temperature rise

$$
\theta(x,t)=a(t)\sin(\pi x/L).
$$

It vanishes at both ends. Substituting into
$\partial_t\theta=\alpha\partial_{xx}\theta$ gives

$$
a'=-\alpha(\pi/L)^2a,
\qquad a(t)=a(0)\exp[-\alpha(\pi/L)^2t].
$$

Thus this mode decays with time constant $L^2/(\pi^2\alpha)$. A mode with twice
the spatial frequency decays four times faster. Diffusion smooths small spatial
features quickly. This analytic slab calculation explains the role of
capacity; it is a different initial-boundary-value problem from the heated
square we run below.

## A uniform-temperature model needs its own assumption

If internal conduction is sufficiently fast compared with boundary exchange,
you may approximate an entire body by one temperature. With uniform body
temperature $T$, constant bath temperature $T_\infty$, and surface exchange
coefficient $h$, its energy account is

$$
c_vV\frac{\mathrm dT}{\mathrm dt}+hA_s(T-T_\infty)=sV.
$$

With no source, the decay time is $c_vV/(hA_s)$. This connects to the
[scalar decay model](/learn/mathematical-modeling/ordinary-differential-equations/).
Comparing internal and surface resistances suggests checking
$\mathrm{Bi}=hL_c/k$, with $L_c=V/A_s$. A small value supports—but does not
by itself quantify the error of—the uniform-temperature approximation.

The heated square has fixed cold boundaries and a spatial temperature profile.
Its single free coefficient on a coarse mesh is the height of a spatial hat,
not the uniform temperature of the whole body. Having one unknown does not
make the two physical models identical.

## Derive the spatially discrete warming equation

Retain the same center hat $\phi$ and write $T_h=300+\theta(t)\phi$.
Testing the transient equation with $\phi$ gives

$$
M\dot\theta+K\theta=F,
\qquad M=\int_\Omega c_v\phi^2\,\mathrm dA.
$$

For square side length $L$, the previous chapter's integrals give
$K=8k/3$ and $F=sL^2/4$. Our square has $L=1$ m. On one cell,

$$
\int_{\mathrm{cell}}\phi^2\,\mathrm dA
=\frac{1}{h^4}\left(\int_0^h\xi^2\,\mathrm d\xi\right)^2
=\frac{h^2}{9}.
$$

Four cells with $h=L/2$ give $M=c_vL^2/9$, carrying units J/(m K).
For $k=1$ W/(m K), $c_v=1$ J/(m³ K), and $s=1$ W/m³:

$$
\theta(t)=\frac FK\left(1-e^{-Kt/M}\right)
=\frac{3}{32}\left(1-e^{-24t}\right)\,\mathrm K,
$$

where the numerical $t$ is in seconds. This is exact in time for the four-cell
spatial approximation. Its time constant is $M/K=1/24$ s. Increasing capacity
slows warming but leaves $F/K$, the eventual temperature rise, unchanged.
The matrix form and time integration of heat equations are treated in
[Bathe, Lecture 20, pp. 3–4](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/2-092-finite-element-analysis-of-solids-and-fluids-i-fall-2009/de3fe8a64f95eb82acd0cd3a279972a4_MIT2_092F09_lec20.pdf).

## Backward Euler changes the time history

Backward Euler evaluates conduction at the new time:

$$
M\frac{\theta_n-\theta_{n-1}}{\Delta t}+K\theta_n=F.
$$

Solve this algebraic equation:

$$
\theta_n=\frac{M}{M+K\Delta t}\theta_{n-1}
+\frac{F\Delta t}{M+K\Delta t}.
$$

Subtract the steady value $\theta_\infty=F/K$. The deficit is multiplied each
step by $r=M/(M+K\Delta t)$. With $\theta_0=0$,

$$
\theta_n=\frac FK(1-r^n).
$$

For $\Delta t=1/24$ s and unit material parameters, $r=1/2$. Before running,
calculate the three center temperatures:

| Step | Time [s] | Center temperature [K] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 0 | 300 |
| 1 | 1/24 | 300.046875 |
| 2 | 1/12 | 300.0703125 |
| 3 | 1/8 | 300.08203125 |

These are values of the recurrence, not values of the continuous-time
exponential. At the first step the exponential gives a rise
$(3/32)(1-e^{-1})$, which exceeds the Backward Euler rise. The step is stable
but coarse compared with the warming time.

## Read and run the storage model

Return to the
[maintained source listing](/learn/heat-transfer/heated-body-in-eqiora/#express-those-equations-in-eqi).
The `TransientHeatedBody` entry makes four changes to the physical declaration:

- `state temperature` marks a quantity with evolution;
- `initial` assigns its starting temperature;
- the parameter `capacity` has units J/(m³ K); and
- `storage capacity * temperature` contributes its rate to the balance.

The runner binds `capacity` to `1.0`, selects
`eqiora.time.BackwardEuler(step_s=1/24)`, creates the initial State, and requests
three accepted steps. Run it from the Get started working folder:

```bash
.venv/bin/python eqiora-source/examples/heated-body/run.py
```

Inspect the printed time and the center value in each accepted State. All eight
exterior coefficients remain 300 K. The center approaches the steady value
from below. The

## Separate two experiments

**Physical change:** double capacity, keeping heating, conductivity, and step
fixed. The steady value is unchanged, but $r$ becomes $2/3$ and the first rise
is $1/32$ K. More energy is needed for a given temperature change.

**Numerical change:** restore capacity and halve the step. Compare at the same
physical time, so use twice as many steps. At $t=1/24$ s, two steps of
$1/48$ s give $r^2=(2/3)^2=4/9$, and a rise of $5/96$ K. This is closer to
the continuous-time value than the single coarse step. Comparing both runs
after one step would compare different physical times.

## Exercises

1. Derive the recurrence for arbitrary positive $M,K,\Delta t$ and constant
   $F$. Why does the deficit always decrease without changing sign?
2. For unit parameters and twice the capacity, calculate the first three
   center rises at the original step size.
3. Derive forward Euler for the same one-coefficient system. When does its
   homogeneous error decay, and when does it decay without alternating sign?
4. Why does shrinking the time step not remove the four-cell spatial error?

<details>
<summary>Solution hints</summary>

1. $0<r=M/(M+K\Delta t)<1$.
2. Use $(3/32)[1-(2/3)^n]$ K.
3. The error multiplier is $1-K\Delta t/M$. Decay requires
   $0<K\Delta t/M<2$; nonnegative decay requires $0<K\Delta t/M\le1$.
   This is a mathematical comparison of methods; the runner uses Backward Euler.
4. The time integrator approaches the ODE defined by the fixed spatial basis.
   Increasing temporal resolution cannot add missing spatial variation.

</details>
