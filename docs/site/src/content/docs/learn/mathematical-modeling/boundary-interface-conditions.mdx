## A domain is not isolated by default

Interior equations describe behavior inside a region. Boundary conditions say
how that region meets its environment; interface conditions say how two modeled
regions meet each other. These relations are part of the mathematical model,
not settings to leave for a solver to invent.

## Learning outcomes

After this chapter, you should be able to:

- distinguish prescribed trace data from prescribed normal flux or traction;
- read the standard fluid and solid boundary components;
- explain continuity and action/reaction at an interface; and
- diagnose missing or contradictory boundary assumptions.

## Can the same wall have two different solutions?

The source-free steady slab obeys $T''=0$, so its general solution is $T=a+bx$.
Two independent constants remain. Prescribing $T(0)=T_h$ and $T(L)=T_c$
determines both, giving the familiar linear profile. If instead the left
surface is held at $T_h$ and the right is insulated, $T'(L)=0$ forces $b=0$:
the whole slab reaches $T_h$. The interior material and balance are unchanged;
the environment changed the answer.

A boundary condition expresses how the surroundings act on the system. It is
part of the physical question, even if software presents it in a different
panel from the interior equations.

## Values, fluxes, and exchange

For Fourier conduction, outward heat flux is
$j_n=-\kappa\nabla T\cdot\boldsymbol n$. Three common idealizations are:

| Boundary statement | Physical picture | Mathematical data |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $T=T_b$ | a controlled-temperature reservoir | prescribed temperature |
| $j_n=q_b$ | a controlled outward heat flux | prescribed normal flux |
| $j_n=h(T-T_\infty)$ | exchange with a surrounding fluid | a relation between flux and temperature |

The last is a Robin condition: the surface temperature is solved rather than
prescribed. The transfer coefficient $h$ has units $\mathrm{W/(m^2K)}$.
An insulated surface uses $q_b=0$; it does not require zero tangential gradient.
The terms “essential” and “natural” below refer to a chosen variational
formulation, so their physical meaning should always be stated too.

For an illustrative slab with $T(0)=T_h$ and convection at $x=L$, the steady
heat flux is

$$
j=\frac{T_h-T_\infty}{L/\kappa+1/h}.
$$

Derive this by adding the slab drop $jL/\kappa$ and surface-to-room drop $j/h$.
As $h$ grows, the surface approaches the room temperature. As $h$ tends to zero,
heat exchange vanishes and the slab approaches $T_h$. These limits connect the
boundary law back to the thermal-resistance network.

## Trace and flux form a boundary pair

An essential condition prescribes a field trace, such as velocity or
displacement. A natural condition prescribes its conjugate flux, such as
traction. The names describe mathematical roles; whether a condition is
physically appropriate depends on the modeled boundary.

## Interfaces exchange equal and opposite action

At an interface between compatible regions, the trace variable is continuous
and the outward fluxes balance. With outward normals defined separately for
the two regions, a compact statement is

$$
\operatorname{tr}u_1 = \operatorname{tr}u_2,
\qquad
f_1 + f_2 = 0.
$$

The first relation is compatibility. The second is action/reaction. Conserving
ports let components expose this pair without deciding in advance which side
computes the trace and which computes the flux.

## Join two materials without creating energy

At a perfectly bonded thermal interface with no surface storage, temperature
is continuous and outward fluxes balance:

$$
T_1=T_2,\qquad j_{n,1}+j_{n,2}=0.
$$

The normals are opposite. The same rightward physical heat flux therefore has
opposite outward signs on the two regions. If conductivities differ, normal
temperature gradients generally differ too: continuity of temperature does not
mean continuity of its derivative.

The resistance calculation from the algebraic chapter now has a continuum
interpretation. Each layer obeys Fourier's law and energy balance; the interface
adds compatibility and exchange. For finite contact resistance per area $r_c$,
a temperature jump can occur, with rightward flux $j=(T_1-T_2)/r_c$. That is a
changed interface model, while conservation of total heat remains intact.

In mechanics, the corresponding pair is displacement or velocity and traction.
Which trace should be continuous depends on the physics: a perfectly bonded
solid interface prevents separation and slip, while a frictional contact needs
a different law. Recognizing this distinction is what lets you judge whether a
bundled boundary component fits your problem.

## A boundary condition can leave a freedom

If both ends of a source-free slab prescribe heat flux, first check that the
total outward power is zero. Otherwise no steady solution exists. Even when
fluxes balance, adding a constant to $T$ leaves its gradient unchanged, so the
absolute temperature is undetermined without another reference or constraint.
For two insulated ends, every constant temperature is a steady solution.

A similar freedom appears in an unloaded elastic body: translating or rotating
it rigidly need not change its strain. Boundary conditions must remove the
unwanted rigid motions if a unique displacement is required. A solver cannot
supply the missing physical reference.

## Choose conditions from the phenomenon

A wall may justify no slip; a free solid surface may justify zero traction; an
outlet may justify a prescribed normal pressure. These are different physical
assumptions even when they use the same boundary support machinery. A complete
set must constrain the intended problem without prescribing incompatible data.

## Deliberate failure: fix and free the same quantity

Suppose the same solid face is declared both fixed and subject to a prescribed
nonzero displacement. Each relation can be dimensionally valid on its own, but
together they contradict one another. Conversely, omitting every condition
that removes rigid translation can leave an elasticity problem undetermined.

Count and interpret the boundary freedoms before choosing a realization. A
successful syntax check cannot decide whether the physical boundary story is
complete.

## Exercises

1. Classify no slip, fixed displacement, traction free, and normal pressure as
   trace or flux conditions.
2. Explain why outward tractions on two sides of an interface sum to zero
   rather than being textually identical.
3. Assign conditions to the inlet, outlet, walls, and cylinder in the checked-in
   steady-flow example. State the physical assumption behind each choice.
4. For an elastic body in two dimensions, explain why traction-free boundaries
   alone do not remove rigid translation.
5. Propose one fluid–solid interface pair: which trace quantity should be
   compatible, and which actions should balance?

### Exercise guidance

For exercise 1, no slip and fixed displacement prescribe traces; traction-free
and prescribed normal traction constrain flux variables. For exercise 2, draw
the two outward normals before writing the sum. For exercise 4, a constant
displacement changes position without changing displacement gradient, so a
stress law cannot determine that translation.

**Closing investigation.** Use two slab layers of thicknesses $L_1,L_2$ and
conductivities $\kappa_1,\kappa_2$, with fixed temperatures at the exterior ends.
Derive the steady heat flux and interface temperature, then recover the network
formula using $R_i=L_i/(\kappa_i A)$. Add a finite contact resistance and predict
which temperature becomes discontinuous. Before running a spatial model, list
one balance, one constitutive assumption, and one interface assumption you would
check against its result.
