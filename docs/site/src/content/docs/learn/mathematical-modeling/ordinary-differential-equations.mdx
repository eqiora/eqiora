## State makes history matter

An ordinary differential equation relates quantities to derivatives with
respect to one independent variable, here time. A first-order initial-value
problem needs both an evolution relation and an initial state. Without the
initial value, the relation describes a family of trajectories rather than one
trajectory.

## Learning outcomes

After this chapter, you should be able to:

- identify state, parameter, derivative relation, and initial value;
- derive the closed form for constant-rate scalar decay;
- keep the time method and output schedule outside the Model;
- run the example with the installed Python package and select a series by
  its `FieldRef`; and
- diagnose a physically wrong sign that remains dimensionally consistent.

## From a rate of cooling to a trajectory

Return to the bead in chapter 1. Its heat capacity $C$ stores energy, and its
conductance $G$ transfers energy to a room at fixed $T_\infty$. The balance
$C\dot T+G(T-T_\infty)=0$ predicts a **rate**, but our thermometer asks for a
**temperature at a later time**. Integrating the rate equation bridges those
questions. Before calculating, predict three features: the bead stays between
its initial temperature and room temperature, the difference decreases
monotonically, and the cooling slows with time.

Normalize with $x=(T-T_\infty)/(T_0-T_\infty)$ and let $k=G/C$. The resulting
source contains just one state, one rate, and one initial value. It also describes
other first-order relaxation systems after the physical quantities have been
mapped correctly. A shared equation does not make their parameters
interchangeable.

## Derive the decay equation

Let dimensionless state $x(t)$ decay with constant rate $k>0$:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} + kx = 0,
\qquad x(0)=1.
$$

Separating variables and integrating from $0$ to $t$ gives

$$
\int_1^{x(t)}\frac{1}{x}\,\mathrm{d}x
=-\int_0^t k\,\mathrm{d}t,
\qquad
\log x(t)=-kt,
$$

so the solution to compare against is

$$
x(t)=\exp(-kt).
$$

The executable lesson uses $k=1\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ and observes $t=0.25$,
$0.5$, and $1\,\mathrm{s}$. They let us compare the trajectory at a quarter, half, and one
characteristic time.

## What the solution tells us before a run

The characteristic time is $\tau=1/k$. At $t=\tau$, the remaining fraction is
$e^{-1}\approx0.367879$, independent of the unit used to express time. The
half-life is found from $e^{-kt_{1/2}}=1/2$, hence $t_{1/2}=\log(2)/k$.
Repeated equal time intervals multiply $x$ by the same factor; they do not
subtract equal amounts.

For $k=1\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, the analytic values are:

| Time (s) | $\exp(-kt)$ | Interpretation |
| ---: | ---: | --- |
| 0.25 | 0.7788007831 | about 78% remains |
| 0.50 | 0.6065306597 | about 61% remains |
| 1.00 | 0.3678794412 | one characteristic time |

These numbers come from evaluating the analytic solution, not reading a solver
trace. The curve satisfies both $x(0)=1$ and $\dot x=-kx$; checking the initial
value matters because $2e^{-kt}$ satisfies the same differential equation.

Recover physical temperature using
$T(t)=T_\infty+(T_0-T_\infty)e^{-kt}$. With the illustrative bead's
$\tau=100\,\mathrm s$, $T_0=340\,\mathrm K$, and $T_\infty=300\,\mathrm K$,
the predicted temperature after 100 seconds is about $314.715\,\mathrm K$.
Changing to this rate requires changing `rate` to 0.01; the default script's
one-second window then covers only one hundredth of a characteristic time.

## What can we reuse?

`decay.eqi` is already the complete mathematical description for
this small problem. There is no need to hide a one-line evolution law behind a
new component. Reuse its source and run script, then open their definitions when
you need to change the law. In the [circuits path](/learn/circuits-dynamics/),
resistors and sources are larger reusable physical parts, and imports remove
repetition after their equations have been derived.

## Change an assumption before changing a solver

Suppose the room temperature becomes $T_\infty(t)$. The energy balance remains
$C\dot T+G(T-T_\infty(t))=0$, but the old normalization no longer produces
unforced decay. For $\theta=T-T_\infty(t)$,

$$
\dot\theta+k\theta=-\dot T_\infty(t).
$$

This follows by differentiating the temperature difference. It explains why a
varying ambient temperature needs a forcing term: cooling a difference and
cooling an absolute temperature are different statements when the reference
moves. Work this extension on paper before editing source.

By contrast, recording a fourth output time leaves the differential equation
unchanged. Tightening an integrator tolerance changes how accurately the
trajectory is approximated. The [numerical-simulation path](/learn/numerical-simulation/)
examines that distinction through time-step error and convergence.

## Deliberate failure: the wrong sign still compiles

Change the residual to:

```eqiora
relation flow {
  derivative(x) - rate * x = 0;
}
```

Both terms still have inverse-time dimension, so compilation can succeed. But
the relation now implies $x(t)=\exp(+kt)$: growth instead of decay, so the
comparison with the decay solution fails. Fix the sign by revisiting the
physical assumption; loosening the tolerance would only hide the error.

### Exercise guidance

Exercise 1 gives $x=x_0e^{-kt}$. For exercise 2, replace $t$ with $2t$ in the
comparison; the source's rate and the comparison must describe the same model.
For exercise 5, several samples on one smooth trajectory do not reveal how
error changes as resolution changes. A stiff system introduces competing time
scales that this scalar example does not contain.

**Inverse question.** Given two positive observations $x(t_1)$ and $x(t_2)$,
derive $k=\log(x(t_1)/x(t_2))/(t_2-t_1)$. Ask what happens if measurement noise
makes the second value larger than the first. The
[inverse-problems path](/learn/inverse-problems/) takes up the distinction
between estimating a parameter and validating the assumed law.
