## A field needs somewhere to live

A scalar such as pressure assigns one value to each point of a region. A
velocity field assigns a spatial vector. Writing only a quantity and its unit
is therefore incomplete: a spatial model must also say which support carries
the field and what shape its values have.

Eqiora separates the semantic support from concrete geometry. A volume says
that a field lives in a region; its boundaries identify the places where the
interior model meets its surroundings. Meshes and discretization choices come
later.

## Learning outcomes

After this chapter, you should be able to:

- distinguish a field's role, support, dimension, and value shape;
- read `grad`, `div`, `trace`, and `normal` by their mathematical roles;
- identify which quantities live in a volume and which are compared on its
  boundary; and
- diagnose an operator applied to the wrong kind of field.

## When one temperature stops being enough

A thick wall can be warm on one side and cold on the other. One state $T(t)$
cannot represent both. Introduce a field $T(x,t)$, with position $x$ across a
slab of thickness $L$. In a steady, uniform, source-free slab with fixed end
temperatures, our prediction is a straight profile:

$$
T(x)=T_h+(T_c-T_h)\frac{x}{L}.
$$

At $x=0$ it returns $T_h$; at $x=L$ it returns $T_c$; at the midpoint it is their
average. Later chapters derive why steady conservation and constant
conductivity imply this shape. For now the field teaches us how a mathematical
object attaches values to places.

| Object | What changes with position? | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scalar field | one number | temperature $T$ |
| Vector field | magnitude and direction | heat flux $\boldsymbol j$ |
| Second-order tensor field | a linear action on a direction | stress $\boldsymbol\sigma$ |

A vector's components depend on a chosen coordinate frame. The physical heat
flow does not change when we rotate the axes. A boundary's outward normal also
depends on geometry: on the left face of this slab it points toward negative
$x$, and on the right face toward positive $x$.

## Work out a gradient before reading syntax

Take $T_h=340\,\mathrm K$, $T_c=300\,\mathrm K$, and $L=0.2\,\mathrm m$.
The derivative is $\mathrm dT/\mathrm dx=-200\,\mathrm{K/m}$. The gradient
points toward increasing temperature, here toward the hot left side. Fourier's
law will give a heat flux pointing the other way. Merely knowing that the
field lies between 300 and 340 K does not reveal this direction.

A field is defined on a domain before we choose sampling points. A mesh is one
way to approximate that domain and its fields; refining it adds numerical
resolution without changing the wall's physical thickness or boundary names.
The following flow source makes those same distinctions for vector velocity and
scalar pressure. Read its declarations first, then return to its balances after
the conservation and constitutive chapters.

## Read spatial operators by where they act

For a scalar field $p$ and vector field $\boldsymbol{u}$:

| Expression | Meaning | Where the result is used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\nabla p$ | spatial rate of change of a scalar | in the volume |
| $\nabla\!\cdot\boldsymbol{u}$ | net outward tendency per unit volume | in the volume |
| $\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{u}$ | interior value restricted to a boundary | on the boundary |
| $\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{n}$ | normal action of a tensor | on the boundary |

The operators also transform dimensions. If velocity has dimension $L/T$,
its divergence has dimension $1/T$. If pressure has dimension force per area,
its gradient has dimension force per volume. Shape and dimension are separate
checks, and both must make sense.

Locate `incompressibility` and `wall_velocity` in the source. The first is an
interior statement; the second compares the boundary trace of interior velocity
with a prescribed zero velocity. A boundary name alone imposes neither relation.

## A spatial equation with a known solution

For a scalar field on the unit square, a useful checking problem is

$$
-\operatorname{div}(\operatorname{grad} u)
=2\pi^2\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y),
\qquad u=0\text{ on all four sides}.
$$

Here $x$ and $y$ are nondimensional coordinates; this is a mathematical test
problem rather than a dimensional heat source. Differentiate twice in each
coordinate: each second derivative contributes
$-\pi^2\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)$. Their negative sum gives the right-hand side.
On every side one sine factor vanishes. Thus both the interior equation and the
boundary values are checked independently.

The source is chosen so the solution is known:
$u(x,y)=\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)$. This is a scalar Poisson problem, not
the cylinder-flow model above. Knowing the answer in advance lets a numerical
comparison measure error instead of relying on a plausible-looking picture.

## Deliberate failure: divergence of a scalar

Replace the incompressibility relation with `div(pressure) = 0`. Pressure is a
scalar, while divergence here expects a spatial vector or tensor. The field is
still on the right support and its unit is still valid, but the operator has no
matching shape. Repair the mathematical statement; changing the mesh or solver
cannot make the expression meaningful.

## Exercises

1. State the support, dimension, and value shape of `velocity` and `pressure`.
2. Explain why `trace(velocity)` belongs in a relation on `walls`, not in the
   volume relation for incompressibility.
3. If temperature has dimension $\Theta$, what dimension does its gradient
   have?
4. Add a named `symmetry` boundary to the support declarations. What physical
   relation would still need to be supplied before the name has meaning?

### Exercise guidance

For exercise 3, $[\nabla T]=\Theta/L$. For exercise 4, a support name identifies
a location; it does not yet impose no flow, a prescribed value, or a stress.
State the physical symmetry and then derive which normal or tangential
quantities it constrains.

**Field check.** For the slab, evaluate $T$ at $x=L/4$ and $3L/4$. If a numerical
profile has the right endpoint values but the wrong midpoint slope, which
interior assumption should you inspect? Boundary agreement alone does not
establish the field equation.
