## Conservation needs material behavior

Conservation constrains storage and transport, but usually does not say how a
material carries flux or stress. A constitutive law supplies that closure. Its
parameters and assumptions describe the modeled material, not a universal
identity.

## Learning outcomes

After this chapter, you should be able to:

- separate a balance law from the constitutive relation that closes it;
- read Newtonian viscosity and isotropic linear elasticity in residual form;
- check the dimensions of material parameters; and
- identify when a constitutive assumption is unsuitable even though its source
  is well typed.

## Why does heat flow down a temperature gradient?

For the stationary slab, conservation gave
$c_v\partial_tT+\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol j=s_v$. Two unknown fields, $T$ and
$\boldsymbol j$, remain. A simple material relation is Fourier's law,

$$
\boldsymbol j=-\kappa\nabla T,\qquad \kappa>0.
$$

For isotropic material, conductivity $\kappa$ is a scalar. The minus sign
makes heat flow toward lower temperature. This is a phenomenological material
law, not a consequence of the divergence theorem. Its elementary slab form is
introduced in [OpenStax, section 1.6](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/1-6-mechanisms-of-heat-transfer).

Return to the slab gradient $-200\,\mathrm{K/m}$. With illustrative
$\kappa=0.1\,\mathrm{W/(m\,K)}$, the predicted flux is
$+20\,\mathrm{W/m^2}$. Increasing conductivity by a factor of two doubles the
flux for the **same prescribed endpoint temperatures**. It does not change
the linear temperature profile in this source-free homogeneous example.
That last qualification matters: with prescribed flux instead, changing
conductivity changes the gradient.

Integrating the constant-gradient law gives
$\dot Q=\kappa A(T_h-T_c)/L$. Therefore the thermal resistance assumed in the
network chapter is $R=L/(\kappa A)$. The component-level relation has now been
derived from the continuum law and geometry. For $L=0.2\,\mathrm m$ and
$A=0.1\,\mathrm{m^2}$, it is $20\,\mathrm{K/W}$ and the 40 K temperature
difference transfers $2\,\mathrm W$.

## Which parts of a linear law are assumptions?

Constant conductivity assumes its variation over the temperature range is
unimportant. Isotropy assumes every direction conducts alike. Locality assumes
the flux at one point responds to the gradient at that point. A laminate can
require a tensor conductivity; a wide temperature interval can require
$\kappa(T)$. Conservation still holds when we replace the constitutive law.

The same separation appears in mechanics: conservation balances forces,
kinematics describes deformation or motion, and a material law turns those
kinematic measures into stress. The following two examples make that pattern
visible without asserting that all materials behave linearly.

## Newtonian viscous stress

For the steady fluid component, the Cauchy stress is represented by

$$
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
= 2\mu\,\operatorname{sym}(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}) - p\boldsymbol{I}.
$$

Velocity gradient has dimension $1/T$. Dynamic viscosity $\mu$ has dimension
$M/(LT)$, so their product has pressure dimension $M/(LT^2)$. The isotropic
pressure term has the same dimension and tensor shape.

The fluid balance places this stress inside a divergence. Find
`dynamic_viscosity` in the source linked below: its declared unit is
`kg / (m * s)`. Its physical value describes a material at the conditions of
interest; choosing it is a modeling decision.

## Isotropic linear elasticity

For small displacement $\boldsymbol{d}$, the solid package uses

$$
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
=2\mu_s\,\operatorname{sym}(\nabla\boldsymbol{d})
+\lambda\,(\nabla\!\cdot\boldsymbol{d})\boldsymbol{I}.
$$

Both strain measures are dimensionless. The Lamé parameters $\mu_s$ and
$\lambda$ therefore have stress dimension. The solid component applies divergence to
this stress and balances it against the body force. Open `IsotropicBalance2d`
in the source below to see the expression in its full context.

The plane-strain and plane-stress components derive different Lamé parameters
from Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio. They share an equilibrium structure
while making different kinematic assumptions.

## Test a material law in a simple motion

Consider plane shear $\boldsymbol u=(\dot\gamma y,0)$ with constant shear rate
$\dot\gamma$. The gradient has one nonzero off-diagonal entry. Symmetrization
places $\dot\gamma/2$ in both off-diagonal entries, so Newtonian shear stress is
$\sigma_{xy}=\mu\dot\gamma$. The factor 2 in the constitutive formula is
necessary: it cancels the half introduced by the symmetric part. A constant
translation has zero gradient and produces no viscous shear stress.

For a solid, $\boldsymbol d=(\varepsilon x,0)$ gives constant small strain
$\varepsilon$ in the $x$ direction. A rigid infinitesimal rotation
$\boldsymbol d=(-\omega y,\omega x)$ has an antisymmetric gradient and zero
symmetric part, so the linear law assigns no strain energy to that motion.
These hand checks tell us more about the formula than a plausible displacement
plot alone.

## Parameters do not validate the material model

Dimensional checking can reject a viscosity supplied in metres or a Young's
modulus supplied in seconds. It cannot establish that a fluid is Newtonian,
that deformation is small, or that an isotropic material adequately describes
the specimen. Those judgments come from the phenomenon, scale, and intended
use of the model.

## Deliberate failure: give viscosity the pressure dimension

If `dynamic_viscosity` is declared as `kg / (m * s ^ 2)`, multiplying it by
`grad(velocity)` produces pressure per time rather than pressure. It can no
longer be subtracted from `isotropic_lift(pressure)`. The dimensional error
points to the parameter declaration; rearranging the momentum equation does
not repair it.

## Exercises

1. Verify the dimensions of both terms in the Newtonian stress.
2. Explain which part of the fluid example is conservation and which part is
   constitutive closure.
3. Show that displacement gradient is dimensionless.
4. Compare the plane-stress and plane-strain parameter expressions in the
   standard package. Which balance and interface structures stay unchanged?
5. Name a material behavior for which isotropic linear elasticity would be a
   poor assumption, even if every unit checks.

### Exercise guidance

For exercise 1, $[\mu]=M/(LT)$ and $[\nabla\boldsymbol u]=1/T$, giving
$M/(LT^2)$ as required. For exercise 4, distinguish suppressed out-of-plane
strain from suppressed out-of-plane stress before comparing parameter
expressions. They are different physical reductions, not two numerical solvers
for an identical problem.

**Boundary-dependent prediction.** Double $\kappa$ in the slab while holding
heat flux, rather than endpoint temperatures, fixed. Derive the new temperature
drop. Then explain why the earlier fixed-temperature experiment had a different
observable response.
