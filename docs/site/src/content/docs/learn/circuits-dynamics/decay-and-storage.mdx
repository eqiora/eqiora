## When the circuit remembers

The divider reaches its algebraic answer without remembering a previous voltage.
A charged capacitor is different: its stored charge is part of the initial
condition. Connect it across a resistor and its voltage falls gradually while
the resistor converts stored electrical energy into heat.

We will derive this discharge on paper and run its normalized scalar equation.
The calculation uses one state, which makes the time scale and energy balance
particularly easy to interpret.

## Charge storage creates a derivative

Let the capacitor voltage $v$ be positive at its upper terminal, and define both
capacitor and resistor currents as entering their upper terminals. For a
constant capacitance $C$, stored charge is $q=Cv$, so

$$
i_C=\frac{\mathrm{d}q}{\mathrm{d}t}
=C\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t}.
$$

The resistor carries $i_R=v/R$. With no external source attached, upper-junction
conservation is $i_C+i_R=0$. Therefore

$$
C\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{v}{R}=0,
\qquad v(0)=v_0.
$$

This ideal RC discharge is treated in
[OpenStax §10.5](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/10-5-rc-circuits).
The sign matters: positive resistor current means the capacitor's into-terminal
current is negative while discharging.

```text
                  shared upper node
              ┌────────●────────┐
              │                 │
          C, v(t)            R, iR ↓
              │                 │
              └────────●────────┘
                  reference node
```

*Conceptual discharge circuit. The capacitor and resistor share their upper and
lower nodes. The resistor current is supplied by decreasing stored charge.*

## Derive the time history before solving

Set $\tau=RC$, with units of seconds. Dividing the ODE by $C$ and separating
variables gives

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{v}=-\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\tau},
\qquad v(t)=v_0e^{-t/\tau}.
$$

For nonzero $v_0$, define $x=v/v_0$. Then

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}+kx=0,
\qquad x(0)=1,\qquad k=\frac{1}{RC}.
$$

Choose $R=1$ kΩ and $C=1$ mF, so $\tau=1$ s and $k=1$ s⁻¹.
The voltage falls to $e^{-1}$ of its initial value after one second. The
following table comes from the exponential formula, not a numerical run:

| Time / s | $x=v/v_0$ | Remaining energy fraction $x^2$ |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 1 | 1 |
| 0.25 | 0.778801 | 0.606531 |
| 0.5 | 0.606531 | 0.367879 |
| 1 | 0.367879 | 0.135335 |

## Recover volts and joules

For $v_0=12$ V, convert the dimensionless result back with $v=12x$ V. At one
second, this predicts about 4.41455 V.

The stored energy is $E=Cv^2/2$. Differentiating and using the ODE gives

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}t}
=Cv\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t}
=-\frac{v^2}{R}=-p_R.
$$

The resistor's positive absorbed power exactly matches the decrease of stored
energy. Here $E_0=(0.001)(12)^2/2=0.072$ J. At one second,
$E=0.072e^{-2}$ J, about 0.009744 J. About 0.062256 J has become heat.
Checking voltage alone would miss a factor-of-two error in an energy formula.

## Recognize the same structure elsewhere

A lumped object cooling toward an environment temperature satisfies
$C_{\mathrm{th}}\dot T=-H(T-T_\infty)$ when its thermal capacity and heat-loss
coefficient $H$ are constant. Its normalized excess temperature decays with
rate $H/C_{\mathrm{th}}$. Compare this with $1/(RC)$: storage divided by a
transport coefficient sets a time scale in both problems.

The [heat-transfer path](/learn/heat-transfer/) explains when uniform
temperature is a reasonable approximation. The scalar trajectory here is the
reduced equation we derived; interpreting it as a capacitor voltage requires
the constant-$R$, constant-$C$, isolated-discharge assumptions stated above.

## Change a sign, a scale, or the observation schedule

Copy the model into your working folder and pass that copy's path to the same
runner. Doubling `rate` halves the time constant. At one second, predict
$x=e^{-2}$ before running.

Changing `+ rate * x` to `- rate * x` instead predicts growth $e^{kt}$. The
units remain consistent, but the isolated discharge would create energy. The
energy argument reveals why this is the wrong physical sign.

Adding more output times changes how densely you observe the solution. It does
not alter the initial condition or introduce a sampled controller. Compare
against the same exponential at every added time.

## Exercises

1. Derive the half-voltage time and half-energy time. Which is shorter?
2. Two RC combinations have the same product $RC$ and the same initial voltage.
   Must their stored energies also agree?
3. Show that $v(t)=V_s+(v_0-V_s)e^{-t/(RC)}$ solves the charging equation
   $C\dot v+(v-V_s)/R=0$ for a constant source.
4. Explain why a state-space view using $v$ and one using $x=v/v_0$ describe the
   same discharge, and what conversion is needed to compare observations.

<details>
<summary>Answer sketches</summary>

1. Voltage halves at $\tau\ln2$; energy halves at $\tau\ln2/2$, because
   energy is proportional to squared voltage.
2. No. The normalized trajectories agree, but $E_0=Cv_0^2/2$ depends on $C$
   separately. For example, doubling $C$ and halving $R$ doubles initial energy.
3. Differentiation gives $C\dot v=-(v_0-V_s)e^{-t/(RC)}/R$, canceling
   $(v-V_s)/R$. At zero time the expression equals $v_0$.
4. They are related by a constant nonzero scale. Multiply $x$ by $v_0$ to compare
   voltages, and by the appropriate energy formula to compare stored energy.

</details>
